Method. Evidence is ordered by time, and the direction of travel is the answer. A well-sourced snapshot does not answer this order.
1. Frame the arc: which thing, over what window, to inform what. "How has this ecosystem evolved" needs a different window than "is this crate still maintained".
2. Before gathering, state the candidate readings: accelerating, stable, declining, consolidating around a winner. Gather to tell them apart, not to collect timeline trivia.
3. Gather evidence that carries dates natively: changelogs, version histories, release cadence, deprecation notices, migration guides, adoption and download curves, star history, conference talk topics by year, hiring posts. Attach a date to every claim. An undated claim cannot go on a timeline.
4. Put it in order and read the curve. Build the real chronology. Mark the inflection points (a breaking release, a maintainer change, a competitor's launch) and the changes of rate: the second derivative is often the story. The whole curve is the deliverable, not the latest point.
5. Argue against it. Survivorship: dead things stop producing sources, so a naive search over-samples the living. Did you look for the ones that died? Is an "accelerating" reading just one hype cycle echoed by many pages (one origin)? Does the window cherry-pick: would a wider one flip the reading?

Delivery. In the summary:
- the arc: a dated chronology with the inflection points;
- the call on where it is heading (accelerating, slowing, stable, declining), with the signals behind it;
- confidence, based on independent corroboration of the trend, not of any single event;
- the periods or signals you could not source.

Avoid. Reporting today's state as a trend. Numbers with no date and no source for the date.
